## MON Oct 19 — Freelance/business lesson #1 — scope changes
Never answer a client's new idea with "sure, no problem."

Saying "no" feels risky.

Saying "sure" is worse. Small yeses add up, the deadline slips, and three weeks later nobody remembers why.

So every new request gets the same reply, in writing:

"Yes, we can add that. Here's what it changes:
→ Cost: +€300
→ Launch: moves from the 14th to the 18th
→ Or we swap it for something on the list, and nothing moves."

(Numbers are an example. The format is the point.)

Then the client decides. Not you.

Why it works:
→ No arguing about what's "in scope". You show the trade-off and let them choose.
→ The client sees the price of an idea before they get attached to it.
→ The written record stops most "but I thought that was included" arguments later.

Business owners: ask your developer to answer every change this way. It's how a budget stays a budget.

## TUE Oct 20 — AI workflow reality — AI-written briefs
An AI-written brief with 40 "must-haves" has no priorities.

Clients now send these. They look great: clear headings, long feature lists, confident language.

The problems are in what's missing:
→ No priorities. Everything is "must have", so nothing is.
→ Vague verbs. "Integrate with our accounting system" can mean a spreadsheet export or three weeks of work.
→ Features nobody asked for. AI tools tend to add them because most briefs include them.
→ Nothing about after launch: who updates it, who pays for hosting, who answers when it breaks.

How to handle one:
1. Ask the client to tag each feature: launch, later, or drop.
2. Turn every vague line into one real example: "When a customer does X, Y happens."
3. Quote the launch list only. Price the rest when it's needed.

Business owners: using AI to draft a brief is a good start. Then cut it to the features that bring in money, and write one real example for each. You'll get faster, clearer quotes and fewer surprises on the invoice.

Developers: have you received an AI-written brief yet? What gave it away?

## THU Oct 22 — Hot take — headless WordPress
Headless WordPress is the wrong choice for most small businesses.

"Headless" means WordPress only stores your content, and a separate app shows it to visitors.

Developers like it. It's modern and fun to build. I get the appeal.

But for a typical business site or store, you now pay for:
→ Two systems to host, update and secure, instead of one.
→ Plugins that no longer work out of the box: forms, SEO tools, page builders, pop-ups. Each needs custom work.
→ Editors losing "preview" until someone builds it back.
→ Fewer developers who can take it over when the original one leaves.

And the speed gain? A well-set-up regular WordPress site with good hosting and caching is fast enough for most businesses.

Where headless is the right call:
→ The same content feeds a website, an app and other screens.
→ A team that will own and maintain the front end for years.
→ Designs or interactions a theme can't do.

My rule: start with standard WordPress. Go headless when you can name the problem it solves, and the budget to keep solving it.

## FRI Oct 23 — Industry commentary — hijacked Shopify apps
This month, hijacked Shopify apps sent shoppers to fake checkouts.

In reports published from October 10, store owners described shoppers clicking "Checkout" and landing on a fake payment page on an outside domain.

The code came through third-party apps. HulkApps said an exploit introduced "unauthorized configurations" into its Hulk Product Options app. Selleasy said an unauthorized party accessed an internal tool. HulkApps, Swym Wishlist Plus and Selleasy were removed from the Shopify App Store.

Shopify hasn't commented publicly. Reports point to app scripts on the store page, not to Shopify's own checkout.

What it means for store owners:
→ Many apps run code on your storefront, sometimes right next to the "Checkout" button.
→ Fewer apps, fewer doors. Remove the ones you haven't used this month.
→ Test it yourself: private window, add to cart, click Checkout. The address bar should show your own domain or shopify.com. Anything else: stop and investigate.
→ Turn on 2-step login for your store and app accounts. It won't stop a hacked app vendor, but it closes the most common way in.

What it means for developers:
→ Keep a list of every third-party script that loads near cart and checkout, and who can change it.
→ Alert when the cart sends people to a domain you don't know.
→ Same risk with WordPress plugins and every "just paste this snippet" tool.

Store owners: how many apps are installed on your store? How many do you actually use?

Source: developer Roman Zipp's running timeline on his blog, which collects the vendors' statements.

## SAT Oct 24 — Light engagement — fixed price or hourly
Fixed price or hourly? Pick one.

Hiring: which do you prefer to pay?
Freelancing: which do you prefer to charge?

Example: a job estimated at 20 hours.
Fixed: quoted at about 25. The extra 5 is the cushion for risk.
Hourly: costs 20, or 35 if surprises show up.

Fixed moves the risk to the freelancer. Hourly keeps it with you.

My pick: fixed per milestone, hourly for work nobody can define yet.

## SUN Oct 25 — Code lesson — BETWEEN misses the last day
This report looks right. It's missing a whole day of sales.

A common date bug, and why it matters even if you never write code.

The query asks for October's orders:

WHERE created_at BETWEEN '2026-10-01' AND '2026-10-31'

Looks fine. But created_at usually stores a date and a time.

In a database like MySQL, '2026-10-31' means midnight at the start of October 31. So every order placed after 00:00:00 that day is left out.

No error. The total just looks a bit low, and nobody checks.

Example math: a store doing €31,000 in October, spread evenly, loses about €1,000 from the report. If the last day was a sale, much more.

The fix: include the start, exclude the next start.

WHERE created_at >= '2026-10-01' AND created_at < '2026-11-01'

The lesson, for everyone:
→ "Between" in plain English includes the last day. In a database, it may not.
→ Check a report against a number you trust, like your payment provider's sales total, at least once.
→ When a number is "a bit low", check the edges first: first day, last day, midnight.

Business owners: when did you last compare your sales report with your payment provider's sales total for the same dates?
